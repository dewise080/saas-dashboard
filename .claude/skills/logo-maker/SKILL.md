# Logo Maker

A logo is an argument about what the product is. Understand the product before drawing anything.

## Phase 0 — Understand the Product
Write answers to five questions:
1. What does the product do in one sentence a stranger understands?
2. Who is it for?
3. What is the ONE idea the mark must carry?
4. What is the name really saying?
5. What would the obvious lazy logo be, so we can reject it?

Then list 4–6 nouns or verbs from the product world. Cross out category clichés.

## The Memory Test
Every concept must pass:
- One breath: explain it in one short phrase.
- From memory: someone can roughly sketch it after one viewing.
- Abstract but anchored: it may be abstract, but the meaning is real.
- Survives 16px: the silhouette still reads as a favicon.

## Craft Principles
- Free-standing symbol first; app-tile containers are application treatments, not the logo itself.
- Form does the work, not effects.
- Use a small number of strong shapes.
- Prefer one clear accent color after the black-and-white form works.
- A letterform can be powerful only when it is transformed by the product's visual grammar.
- Verify visually at multiple sizes; do not trust SVG/code structure alone.

## Generate Many, Ship Few
Create 8–12 concept ideas, each meaningfully different. Cut aggressively. Do not present weak concepts just to fill a grid.

The final review should contain no more than 3–5 strong survivors.

## Anti-Slop Rejection List
Reject a concept immediately if any are true:
- plain system-font initial with no ownable construction
- rainbow or multi-stop gradient doing the identity work
- generic speech bubble, robot, globe, rocket, lightbulb, gear, sparkles, or magic wand
- meaningless geometric shapes
- glossy app tile masquerading as a logo
- more detail than survives at favicon size
- symbol could belong to any AI/SaaS product
- explanation is vague branding language instead of a product truth

## Wordmark Discipline
The wordmark is part of the identity, not an afterthought.
Explore distinctive typographic registers and custom letter treatments. Avoid relying on default startup typography. Test spacing, weight, optical balance, and lockup proportions.

## Runs It All exploration prompts
For Runs It All, derive concepts from product truths rather than the letter R alone.

Explore:
1. MANY → ONE: multiple paths or operations resolving into one controlled system.
2. CONTINUITY: a form that continues without interruption, representing a business that keeps operating.
3. ORCHESTRATION: independent parts moving in coordinated rhythm.
4. COMMAND: one central control point influencing many outcomes without looking militaristic.
5. COVERAGE: all customer touchpoints accounted for without using a generic circle of icons.
6. LEVERAGE: a small input creating a larger coordinated output.
7. CALM CONTROL: complexity outside, simplicity inside.
8. LETTERFORM TRANSFORMATION: only if R, RIA, or another letter construction can embody one of the concepts above in a way unique to this brand.

For every concept write:
- concept name
- one-sentence meaning
- why the shape is specific to Runs It All
- what emotion it should create
- what visual cliché it deliberately avoids
- expected favicon behavior

Do not render final color until the strongest forms survive black-and-white review.
